Abstract
The exponential growth of cloud computing has made data center energy consumption a critical environmental challenge, currently accounting for nearly 2% of global electricity use. Despite this, existing cloud infrastructures rely on static, carbon-agnostic scheduling that fails to consider the significant temporal and spatial variability of grid carbon intensity. This paper proposes and evaluates the Context-Aware and Adaptive Resource Allocation (CARA) architecture, a system designed to decouple compute growth from environmental impact. The methodology involves an event-driven architecture that integrates real-time Marginal Emissions Rate (MER) forecasts into a Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO) engine to perform joint spatio-temporal shifting of asynchronous workloads. To evaluate this framework, a prototype was developed using Python 3.9, the ElectricityMap API for real-time grid data, and an emulated cloud execution model. The empirical results demonstrate that CARA achieves a 75.7% reduction in carbon footprint compared to carbon-agnostic baseline schedulers. This significant reduction was achieved under a 12-hour MaxLatency constraints for asynchronous workloads, specifically by exploiting the carbon intensity arbitrage between fossil-fuel-intensive local grids and globally optimal regions during peak renewable penetration windows. Furthermore, the system maintains a negligible scheduling overhead of just 0.54 s and validates a Spatio-Temporal Efficiency Ratio that quantifies the environmental return on latency. These findings confirm that carbon-aware scheduling is a high-impact, low-cost architectural principle that can effectively mitigate the environmental burden of digital infrastructure.
Introduction
The rapid expansion of cloud computing, large-scale data analytics, and AI workloads has led to an accelerating rise in global data-center electricity consumption, reaching approximately 415 TWh in 2024 [4]. This trend is not merely a technical concern but a significant societal challenge, as data centers now represent about 1.5–1.7% of global electricity demand, with projections suggesting a doubling of this demand by 2030 [14]. The resulting greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from this infrastructure have become a focal point for corporate sustainability reporting. Addressing this energy consumption is vital for global climate-mitigation goals, as the regional concentration of compute power imposes immense pressure on local grids and increases avoidable carbon footprint of digital services.
According to Cozzi et al. [4], data centers are projected to become a dominant driver of electricity demand growth across major economies, particularly in the United States and Japan, where they may account for nearly half of the total growth through 2030. In the United States alone, recent energy usage reports indicate that data center power requirements are outpacing initial efficiency gains as AI integration deepens [13]. This underscores the scale and urgency of the problem, highlighting that the expansion of digital services is directly linked to the stability and the aggregated emissions of national power systems.
A key concept in addressing this challenge is Carbon-Aware Computing, which involves aligning digital demand with the availability of clean energy. Traditional scheduling mechanisms are carbon-agnostic, meaning they dispatch workloads immediately without considering the carbon intensity of the electricity being consumed. This is problematic because grid cleanliness fluctuates dramatically; carbon intensity can vary by as much as 460 gCO₂/kWh within a single day depending on the energy mix [4].
Connecting to the issue of grid variability is the concept of Marginal Emissions Rate (MER), which measures the emissions impact of an incremental change in electricity demand. While many systems utilize average carbon intensity for historical accounting, MER is prioritized in this work because it identifies the specific "peaking" power plants that must ramp up or down to meet software loads, thereby capturing the true marginal impact of scheduling decisions. By utilizing MER forecasts, systems can perform Temporal and Spatial Shifting to move workloads to times or regions where renewable energy penetration is highest. While studies have shown that shifting asynchronous workloads can reduce a task’s carbon footprint by approximately 5% while respecting deadlines [15], the industry still lacks a standardized, automated architecture to implement this at scale.
To the best of our knowledge, joint spatio-temporal optimization has not yet been included as a standard control-plane feature in general-purpose cloud integrators. A significant quantitative gap remains in existing literature; for instance, while frameworks like CarbonFlex achieve a 57.5% reduction in emissions through temporal shifting [6], they remain spatially constrained. By failing to integrate spatial mobility, existing systems miss an estimated 20–25% of additional abatement potential available through regional carbon intensity arbitrage. So far, most studies have focused either on purely temporal shifting within a single cluster or purely spatial placement without dynamic forecast integration [10]. Furthermore, many existing solutions operate at an aggregate load level rather than providing per-task latency and carbon footprint targets, which often makes these methods unpractical for diverse, asynchronous enterprise workloads.
The fundamental problem addressed in this research is that existing cloud orchestration frameworks treat computational resources as geographically and temporally static, leading to significant avoidable emissions because they cannot simultaneously exploit spatial and temporal arbitrage based on marginal grid signals. Consequently, the primary research objective is to develop and evaluate a modular, automated architecture that leverages real-time MER forecasts to perform joint spatio-temporal optimization, thereby minimizing a task's carbon footprint while strictly adhering to application-specific latency constraints.
Unlike prior carbon-aware frameworks such as CarbonFlex and CA-SDK, which primarily provide policy guidance or developer-level abstractions, CARA introduces an end-to-end, event-driven scheduling architecture that embeds real-time marginal emissions intelligence directly into the orchestration layer. CARA uniquely formulates workload placement as a multi-objective optimization problem, balancing carbon footprint reduction, latency tolerance, and scheduling overhead, and introduces a novel Spatio-Temporal Efficiency Ratio to quantify the environmental return on execution delay. To evaluate the impact of real-time environmental metrics on cloud orchestration, this study formally tests the following hypothesis:
•
Null Hypothesis (
): The integration of real-time environmental metrics into a Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO) framework does not result in a significant reduction in carbon footprint compared to carbon-agnostic baselines, or it fails to maintain compliance with established Service Level Agreements (SLAs).
•
Alternative Hypothesis (
): The CARA architecture, utilizing a MOO framework, yields a statistically significant carbon reduction, exceeding a 50% threshold, while strictly adhering to SLA-defined latency constraints.
We evaluate these hypotheses through a prototype that simulates diverse batch workloads across multiple AWS regions, specifically targeting the arbitrage between coal-heavy and renewable-rich grids.
The data clearly shows that the CARA architecture successfully reduced the carbon footprint of model-training workloads by 75.7% compared to traditional baseline schedulers. This optimization was achieved under a 12-hour max latency window with a negligible performance overhead, proving that the system can effectively convert slack time in asynchronous tasks into verifiable emissions savings. Additionally, the system demonstrated technical robustness by generating cryptographically signed execution manifests, ensuring that green computing claims are auditable.
There are several contributions this study makes; primarily, this study proposes a novel modular, cloud-agnostic architecture that uniquely integrates MER ingestion, MOO-based decision-making, and deferred execution using native cloud schedulers like AWS EventBridge [1]. This research advances the understanding of how Carbon Intensity Arbitrage can be automated within the software lifecycle. By treating the carbon footprint as a first-class scheduling constraint, CARA provides a practical blueprint for the next generation of sustainable distributed systems.

Result and discussion
The evaluation of the CARA architecture focuses on the optimization magnitude, the computational cost of green logic, and the auditability of the resulting decisions. The empirical results, derived from a 3-hour model-training workload simulation across four global AWS regions, validate the feasibility of decoupling compute demand from grid carbon intensity.
Conclusion and future works
This study demonstrates that the Context-Aware and Adaptive Resource Allocation (CARA) architecture successfully decouples compute demand from grid carbon intensity by treating operational slack in asynchronous, delay-tolerant workloads as a flexible environmental asset. Beyond the empirical achievement of a 75.7% reduction in carbon footprint, the research synthesizes a critical shift in cloud orchestration: the transition from "follow-the-data" to "follow-the-renewables" paradigm.
